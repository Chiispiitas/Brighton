# 4A - What do you eat?

Unit 4: Let's Eat! A language lesson on food and drink and countable/uncountable nouns, using traditional breakfast examples. Printed pages 31-32. Eight activities, two recordings.

Do not require students to introduce there is/are, some/any or containers/measurement language.
8. Personal information from a partner. The printed exchange is a language model, not the partner's answer. Learners then write two complete sentences about the classmate, using known third-person forms. Example: Ana eats eggs for breakfast. She drinks milk. Accept consistent invented personal information if a learner prefers it; no learner must disclose food access, health or religious practice.
Extra Practice: three original sentences about family breakfast habits. Example: I eat bread for breakfast. My sister eats an egg. We drink tea. Content is personal; learners may describe an imagined family.

## Teaching sequence and language limits

Match twelve clear cutouts to everyday food/drink nouns, then encounter the same language in two short breakfast descriptions. Compare singular an egg, plural eggs, and uncountable bread in activity 4 before reading the grammar box. Its singular/plural examples and article-free mass-food examples support the sort and article practice. Rice and eggs are worked examples in the sorting table. Explain that a/an goes with a singular countable noun; plural countable nouns and the uncountable food meanings here take neither a nor an. Recycle the earlier sound-based a/an distinction: an egg/an apple, a banana. Do not add a new adjective-order explanation.

For early A1, fish, cheese, bread, rice, milk, tea, coffee and juice are introduced in their uncountable food/drink senses. Do not teach that these words are uncountable in every possible context. A coffee meaning a serving, cheeses meaning varieties and fish as animals are outside this lesson's scope. Countable eggs, apples, bananas and tomatoes supply plural contrast. Eat is introduced by the title and repeated input; drink and present-simple questions/third-person forms recycle Unit 3. Traditional breakfasts provide cultural context without claiming that all people in a country eat the same meal.

The continuation-page picnic supplies clear visible quantities for open description. The final pair task has a genuine information gap: the partner supplies unknown personal information, followed by two open response lines. It remains an A language lesson, rather than a D writing-skills lesson. No like/dislike/object pronouns (4B), too much/many/enough or recipes (4C), or because/so (4D) is required. No food, health, portion or diet recommendation is made.
