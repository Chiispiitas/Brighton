# 3C - Where do you go?

Unit 3: My Life. Focus: present-simple questions with where, when, who ... with and what time; adjectives for places. Builds on 3A affirmative routines, 3B do/does, days of the week, 2B times, and 2D adjective position. Does not introduce frequency adverbs, before/after, why/because or a general places-in-town syllabus.

## Answers

1. Palm Park **A**; Beat Café **B**; Cloud Café **C**. The café price labels provide exact evidence for B/C; do not infer price from décor or assume a photograph proves sound levels.
2. beautiful, quiet, busy, noisy, cheap, expensive. Learners locate all six in the reviews. Quiet/noisy and cheap/expensive are opposite pairs; beautiful and busy are not opposites. Use the empty park, people in the café and the explicit $2/$8 prices as context. Briefly clarify the new target meanings in the learners' language where needed. There is no universal absolute threshold for “cheap” or “expensive”; these two prices establish the classroom comparison.
3. Ravi goes to **Cloud Café**.
4. 1 **Where**; 2 **Who**; 3 **When**; 4 **What time**. Students select the question word using the visible answer, not an abstract grammar label.
5. 1 **Where**; 2 **When**; 3 **Who**; 4 **What time**. Capitalize the start of each written question.
6. **Eva: Beat Café** ($2 coffee is within her $3 budget). **Ben: Palm Park** (explicitly quiet, with free admission). **Sol: Cloud Café** (explicitly beautiful, with $8 coffee within her $10 budget). Amounts labeled max. beside names are spending limits, not the prices of the chosen place. Coffee is part of Eva/Sol's brief, so the park is not an answer merely because it is free. Do not infer quietness or prices from décor; use the written review and price evidence.
7. Personal information and recommendation. Ask When do you go? Who do you go with? and the two short preference questions. Record the classmate's real answers, then choose a suitable place from the source reviews or a mutually familiar place. Short preference answers are enough; no because or new preference grammar is required. The information is unknown until the partner answers. Quiet and cheap can lead to a free park if coffee is not requested; if none of the source places fits, accept a justified local alternative. Do not force every classmate into a preset answer.
8. An independently worded personal place review. Four full writing lines allow students to choose what to say rather than complete sentence stems. Encourage a place description with suitable adjectives and any useful details about visits, companions, day or time; do not require one fixed structure or all those details. Accept a positive, mixed or negative review. The three reviews on page 27 supply models. Example only: Central Park is beautiful and quiet. I go to Central Park on Sunday with my family. We go at nine. This is one possible response, not a compulsory frame.
After exchanging the independently written reviews in activity 8, partners choose a place they would visit. Extra Practice: write a useful short review for a family member, selecting a suitable place; no real-world posting or contact is required.

## Teaching sequence

The review feature introduces target adjectives in a recognizable setting. Locate and pronounce them in activity 2, then use the Adjectives vocabulary reference for further practice. Recycle adjective location from 2D when needed. Use the 3.7 conversation as direct evidence for question words and do/does. Read it once for Ravi's place, then connect each question to its answer in activity 4. The grammar focus on page 28 clarifies the pattern; activity 5 checks question meaning; activity 6 makes students use the review evidence to recommend places. Adjective location and a/an can be recycled orally from 2D while checking the reviews. Activity 7 uses unknown personal preferences and visits to produce a recommendation. Third-person does questions remain modeled in the source, discovery, grammar and activity 5; personal do questions are now used for an actual choice. The final review transfers the language to a place they actually go to.

Only the four supported question frames are production targets. Who does ... go with? asks about a companion; do not broaden this into subject questions such as Who goes...? Avoid formula-like discovery prompts. Use the visible sentences, bold forms and answers.
