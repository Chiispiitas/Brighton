# Grammar practice — Units 1–4

These keys refer to each unit’s two pages and the lettered tasks printed there. Choices, transformations and open production are distinguished below. Contractions and full forms are both acceptable in open answers unless a specific form is requested. Original tasks and concept diagrams; no exercise copied from a reference coursebook.

## Unit 1 — People and classes (first page)

**A:** 1 am; 2 are; 3 is; 4 are.

**B:** 1 is; 2 is; 3 are.

**C:** 1 Is Nina a teacher? No, she is not. / No, she isn’t. 2 Are Ben and Mia in Class 2? Yes, they are. Do not infer additional personal details beyond the cards.

**D:** Two complete sentences with a matching subject and am/is/are. One must be true and the other false according to the writer; the partner identifies the false sentence. Real or invented details are acceptable if the writer can explain the intended answer.

## Unit 1 — Words that work together (second page)

**A:** 1 a; 2 an; 3 a; 4 an. The explanation is sound-based; avoid presenting a/an as an exception-free letter rule.

**B:** Rosa: a. Dan: an.

**C:** 1 I’m a student. 2 You’re my friend. 3 He’s a doctor.

**D:** 1 She is my teacher. 2 It is a good job.

**E:** Hello, I am Sara Cruz. I am from Canada. Accept a full stop after Hello in place of the comma when the rest is correctly capitalized. The apostrophe/contraction is not required here. Email addresses preserve their supplied spelling and case.

## Unit 2 — Near, far and already known (first page)

**A:** 1 this; 2 these; 3 that; 4 those. The printed distance clues establish each answer.

**B:** 1 This is; 2 Those are; 3 These are.

**C:** a; an; The; The.

**D:** I have a phone. The phone is in my bag. The first change is the consonant-sound article; the second identifies the already introduced phone.

## Unit 2 — Owners and descriptions (second page)

**A:** 1 Ana’s bag; 2 Carlos’s phone; 3 my parents’ shop. The book explicitly models Carlos’s. The plural parents takes a final apostrophe.

**B:** 1 teacher’s; 2 sisters’; 3 Diego’s. The parenthetical one/two-owner clues remove ambiguity.

**C:** 1 a blue bag; 2 two red notebooks; 3 My keys are black.

**D:** 1 The notebook is green. 2 a brown bag.

**E:** A noun phrase with the adjective before the noun and a complete corresponding sentence with the adjective after be. Example: a black phone; My phone is black. In a plural example, the adjective remains unchanged and the verb agrees. No adjective-order hierarchy is assessed.

## Unit 3 — Routines: statements and questions (first page)

**A:** 1 works; 2 have; 3 goes; 4 watch.

**B:** 1 She gets up at six. 2 She has lunch at one. 3 She goes home at five.

**C:** 1 We don’t work on Saturday. / We do not work on Saturday. 2 Leo doesn’t eat breakfast at home. / Leo does not eat breakfast at home.

**D:** 1 Does Mia live near the café? Yes, she does. 2 Do you walk to work? No, I don’t. The main verb remains in its base form after does/doesn’t.

## Unit 3 — Details, frequency and order (second page)

**A:** 1 Where; 2 What time; 3 Who. The instruction supplies these three choices. A learner’s separately phrased question with When and a clock-time answer is meaningful English, but the controlled item asks for the supplied specific expression What time.

**B:** Where do you go on Saturday?

**C:** 1 always drink; 2 is usually; 3 never watch.

**D:** Two true complete sentences. The first uses sometimes before an ordinary verb, e.g. I sometimes walk to work. The second uses am usually, e.g. I am usually at home at six. Real or invented personal details are permitted. Do not require a precise numerical percentage for the visual frequency dots.

**E:** 1 after; 2 before. The diagram explicitly fixes breakfast at 07:00 and walking to work at 07:30.

**F:** One original sentence linking two events in a coherent order with before or after. Example: I drink water after I have breakfast. Accept a noun or a full clause after before/after, as modeled. The answer must remain in the present simple for routine actions.

## Unit 4 — Food and the words after verbs (first page)

**A:** Countable: egg, banana, tomato. Uncountable: water, rice, bread.

**B:** 1 three eggs; 2 rice; 3 an apple; 4 bread.

**C:** 1 it; 2 us; 3 him; 4 them.

**D:** 1 I help her. 2 Our teacher helps us.

## Unit 4 — Enough for everyone (second page)

**A:** 1 too many; 2 not enough; 3 too much.

**B:** 1 We have enough rice. 2 We do not have enough tomatoes. The first is an affirmative amount and the second a shortage; both place enough immediately before the noun. Alternative order is not expected because the task asks for the supplied words.

**C:** 1 because; 2 so.

**D:** I have too many eggs, so I share them. Do you want two? Required features are initial capitals, capital I, a comma before so, a full stop after them and a question mark after two.

**E:** A short comprehensible food-sharing message naming the food and giving a reason for sharing. An accurate because-clause or a reason followed by , so is appropriate. Example: I have too many apples, so I share them. Do you want two? Check appropriate capitalization, a full stop and any question mark. Assess successful meaning and target form rather than a compulsory copied model.

## Scope and source alignment

The practice follows the current Unit 1–4 learner sources. It does not introduce adjective-order rules, an artificial visible information gap or a new listening task. All tasks are print-and-discussion practice and need no unrecorded audio. Diagrams explain the actual concept; no diagram color reveals an uncompleted controlled answer. The extra capitalization heading is linked to the existing 1D writing convention even though the old source had no grammar-bank reference for it.
