## Answers

1. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday. Weekend selections depend on the learner's country and situation; do not impose one universal weekend.
2. Different.
3. Lucía: Tuesday (example) to **Saturday**; home in the evening at **eight / 8:00 p.m.** Ken: Monday to **Friday**; home at **seven / 7:00 p.m.** Maryam: home at **twelve / 12:00 noon on Friday**. 4. 1 **Do**: Do you work on Monday? 2 **doesn't**: Ken doesn't work on Saturday. 3 **go**: He doesn't go home at five.
5. 1 **don't**; 2 **Does**; 3 **go**; 4 **Do**.
6. 1 **Lucía**; 2 **Ken**; 3 **Maryam**. All three clues are verbatim extracts from Track 3.5; replay the audio. Use the original recording, not a synthetic replacement. The twelve o'clock clue refers to Friday; seven means Ken's evening return home.
7. **Sunday.** Lucía works Tuesday-Saturday, Ken Monday-Friday, Maryam Monday-Friday. On Monday Ken and Maryam work; on Saturday Lucía works. Sunday is the only offered day all three are off. Their separate cities make this a remote radio episode, not a physical lunch appointment; no international call time is inferred. Accept, for example, Lucía doesn't work on Sunday. Ken and Maryam don't work on Sunday. One long line is supplied; one accurate compound sentence is sufficient. The Monday exchange is a model of the checking process, not the chosen day.
8. Personal answers. Partners ask about their actual work/school days and choose a feasible day and time for coffee or another familiar shared activity. Learners may say they do not work and discuss school/personal availability instead; never infer that not working means automatically available. If there is no real shared day, accept that outcome rather than force an invented appointment. The printed dialogue is a model, not the partners' answers. Our day and Time record the decision; no survey table is required.
Extra Practice: ask a family member and agree a possible day/time for dinner. An imagined family arrangement is acceptable when real contact is impractical; no external message is required.

## Teaching and listening notes

This receptive-skills lesson follows 3A's affirmative routines. Track 3.4 models the seven days; Track 3.5 supplies the meaning and grammar evidence for the spread. Play the whole episode for exercise 2, replay for exercise 3, then use the exact printed extracts for exercise 4. After a short four-item controlled check, replay for voice recognition in activity 6 and infer a possible new episode day in activity 7. This keeps the B lesson listening-led rather than adding a second block of auxiliary gap-fills. Do not distribute the full transcript before the listening tasks.

The short gist task deliberately allows listening without understanding every word. Natural support phrases in the episode are not test targets. The five new detail gaps have explicit, repeated or contextualized answers; the one o'clock lunch trip is a plausible distractor. Ask students to distinguish lunch from the end of the working day. All day names retain their required capitals, including in circle-the-answer tasks.

For activity 4, first read the two model exchanges. Point to Do you...? and He doesn't go... in the examples, then have learners choose words inside the three complete sentences. Item 1 practises Do with you; item 2 practises doesn't with Ken/he; item 3 practises the base verb go after doesn't. Avoid asking beginners to decode abstract labels such as “Question with you” or “After doesn't”.

Clarify don't = do not and doesn't = does not orally. After doesn't and does, use the base verb, not the 3A third-person -s form. Do not introduce wh-question rules, frequency adverbs, or after/before sequencing here; those belong to 3C/3D. The question about times in the receptive audio recycles earlier What time...? without teaching the next lesson's wider wh-question system.

## Sources, fictionalization and limits

Lucía, Ken and Maryam, their workplaces and their interviews are original fictional coursebook content. The real stock photographs illustrate these fictional roles; the model's real name, nationality, occupation and the photograph's location are not asserted by the story labels. There is no claim that the people pictured gave the interviews or endorse this book. They do not describe all workers in Spain, Japan or the UAE. Lucía's split working day and Ken's longer day illustrate individual routines; neither is evidence that every Spanish person has a siesta or every Japanese person works overtime. Avoid these claims in classroom discussion.

Maryam's example follows the normal UAE **federal government** schedule, not every UAE employer and not Sharjah's local government schedule. The standard hours are Monday–Thursday 7:30–15:30 and Friday 7:30–12:00, with Saturday/Sunday off; holiday, Ramadan, role-specific and flexible arrangements may differ. Her personal arrival/departure times are a simplified teaching example based on those hours.

Sources checked 2026-10-10: [UAE government: public-sector working hours](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/jobs/working-in-uae-government-sector/working-hours-in-the-public-sector), [Federal Authority for Government Human Resources: contact hours](https://www.fahr.gov.ae/en/contact/). They support only the work-schedule detail, not the fictional interview.
