# Vocabulary practice — Units 1–4: revision games and new resources

Current revision, 1 November 2026. These keys supersede the earlier V1–V4 picture-catalog practice draft. The numbered tasks continue across each unit’s pages. Controlled answers, game conditions and open alternatives are distinguished below. All profiles, club notes, room offers and schedules are original classroom fiction. No new recording is claimed.

## Unit 1 — Messages, names and codes (page 93)

**1 — Reply cards:** A Good morning. B Good afternoon. C Good evening. D Goodbye. / See you later. The instruction explicitly asks for morning, afternoon or evening in A–C, so Hello alone does not complete that controlled requirement. In the partner round, the speaker chooses a card privately; the listener identifies it from the spoken response. Change roles. No recording or teacher-only hidden resource is required.

**2 — Name passwords:** A K-I-M, Kim. B L-U-Z, Luz. C S-A-M, Sam. D I-D-A, Ida. The caller spells the letters; the listener writes the name and reports the matching card letter. The independent alternative retrieves two names from memory and spells the learner’s own name. Do not assess whether a name is culturally familiar, only the letters shown/heard.

**3 — Locker codes:** A 4–0–8. B 2–10–6. C 9–1–5. D 7–3–4. Each is a three-number code, not necessarily a three-digit code; ten correctly occupies one large box. A reader chooses privately; the listener covers the written code words and identifies the locker from the spoken numbers. For the invented-code extension, three correctly spelled number words from zero to ten must agree with the partner’s digits and read-back. No addition, subtraction or other arithmetic is assessed.

## Unit 1 — Who belongs where? (page 94)

**4 — Mixed labels:** Camila = first name; Rios = last name; Argentina = country; Salta = city. The learner corrects the field label, not the supplied name/place. Circle either camila@club.example or elliot@club.example; underline the phone number from the same or the other fictional note. Accept either note choice. In the secret-member round, two correct details must identify one of the two members without naming them; the listener spells the name from the selected note. These are invented contact details, not real people’s information.

**5 — Duty board:** 1 chef; 2 receptionist; 3 doctor; 4 farmer; 5 taxi driver; 6 teacher. The numbered source cards establish the task, and the six crossed-out jobs are deliberately assigned incorrectly. Accept a chef, a receptionist, a doctor, a farmer, a taxi driver, a teacher if learners include the indefinite article.

**6 — Job badges:** student (e); office worker (o); salesclerk (e); waiter (e). In the game, the speaker chooses a job privately and gives an appropriate work place; the partner guesses from the clue. More than one job can share a place, so accept a follow-up clarification or an alternative supported by the clue. Examples: a student at school, an office worker in an office, a salesclerk in a clothes store, a waiter in a restaurant. In independent work, two accurate job/place pairs suffice. The game does not require pretending to hold a real job.

## Unit 1 — Send it. Change it. Choose it. (page 95)

**7 — Mail routing, in printed country order:** Argentina 4; the UK 2; Canada 3; the US 1; South Korea 5. Reverse retrieval: Argentinian, British, Canadian, American, South Korean respectively. The nationality is supplied in each postcard’s sender description; the job is recycled supporting language. No celebrity biography or map/flag knowledge is needed.

**8 — Room message:** Our room is small. The building is new. It is calm and cold. Only the four underlined words are changed. The source deliberately establishes a room contrast; it does not claim that old buildings are always busy, new buildings always calm, or any city has a universal climate.

**9 — Club clues:** 1 smart; 2 fun; 3 friendly; 4 famous.

**10 — Room choice:** Either room is acceptable with two source-supported adjective statements. Example A: Room A is big. It is cheap. Example B: Room B is calm. The building is new. A because-clause is not required before Unit 4; simple be statements achieve the task. Preserve adjective meaning and the stated room evidence. Beautiful/good and the cost/size/age/temperature words are available for a meaningful personal choice, not a universal judgment about room quality.

## Unit 2 — The table and the clock (page 96)

**1 — Owners:** Ida: notebook and pen, in either order. Max: umbrella. Luz: wallet. Ida’s blue-color and writing clues together exclude the black phone, clear bottle and brown wallet; these are not facts inferred from stereotyped ownership. The umbrella is identified by the rain clue, and the wallet by the money clue.

**2 — Memory and missing item:** The other three pictured objects are keys, phone and bottle, in any order. Headphones are the missing music item (the h is supplied as a cue). Complete the whole word correctly; an answer after the preprinted h may contain only the remaining letters. Seven distinct object types are shown; do not require a count of the individual keys on the ring. A partner checks after the picture is uncovered.

**3 — Clock corrections:** C shows 6:10, so write six ten. D shows 9:50, so write nine fifty. A seven twenty and B eight forty are already correct. The short hour hand advances proportionately with the minutes. The task is to repair two labels, not match a repeated lesson time list.

**4 — Secret clock:** A 7:20; B 8:40; C 6:10; D 9:50. The caller’s chosen clock must remain private until the listener responds. Either partner may ask for repetition. Independent alternative: cover the captions and write all four times in words, then uncover and check. This does not require a drawing area or an unprovided blank clock.

## Unit 2 — Family messages and color trails (page 97)

**5 — Chat replies:** 1 sister; 2 brother; 3 son; 4 daughter; 5 mother; 6 father. Noa explicitly identifies Mila as mother and Leo as father; gender/role is not inferred from names. He explicitly states that he is their son and Tess their daughter, establishing the sibling relationships.

**6 — Secret person:** Any of Noa, Tess, Mila or Leo can be selected if two accurate family clues identify the person. Mila example: I am Noa’s mother. I am Tess’s mother. No real family disclosure is required. A learner may give shorter but clear noun-phrase clues at this level.

**7 — Color trails:** 1 D (A→B→C→D). 2 H (I→J→F→G→H). 3 J (A→E→I→J). Moves share a side; diagonal moves are excluded. The specified start prevents ambiguity from repeated red/blue cells. Routes collectively retrieve red, blue, yellow, green, orange, black, white, brown, pink and gray. White has an outline so it remains visible on paper.

**8 — Learner trail:** A valid four-color trail includes its start cell and three side-sharing moves. The speaker supplies the start letter and the four colors without showing the route. The listener traces the same route and reports its last letter. If adjacent alternatives create ambiguity, choose another color sequence or ask for clarification. Accept any valid route; there is no single compulsory personal path. Independent writing can record one valid start/color/end route and self-check it.

## Unit 3 — Put the day back together (page 98)

**1 — Photo editor:** A, C, B, D. The visible plan fixes getting up at 6:30, breakfast at 7:00, going to work at 8:00 and television at 8:00 p.m. Use this supplied classroom plan, not assumptions that everybody has the same daily routine. The office-door photo is interpreted together with the explicit plan, not as documentary proof of the woman’s direction of travel.

**2 — Reminders:** 1 lunch; 2 home; 3 dinner; 4 bed. Then say the complete chunks: have lunch; go home; have dinner; go to bed. Combined with the photo-plan task, all eight taught Unit 3 routine chunks are retrieved. The reminders describe the fictional editor’s day and do not impose a universal meal/sleep schedule.

**3 — Private time changes:** The speaker changes exactly two plan times privately and reads the resulting day. The listener identifies both changed actions/times against the public baseline. Accept any two coherent new times, not necessarily a teacher-preferred schedule. Independent alternative: retrieve two routine chunks from the covered plan and check spelling/meaning. This is a genuine private change rather than a visible answer table labeled as an information gap.

## Unit 3 — A day and a place that work (page 99)

**4 — Meeting calendar:** Friday is the only day Ari and Bea are both free. Correct Tusday to Tuesday and Saterday to Saturday. Other printed day names are already correct. This is a fixed invented schedule, not a live claim about any learner’s availability.

**5 — Personal calendar game:** Each partner privately chooses three busy days. Find and name one day when neither is busy. The answer must agree with both private notes. Learners may invent availability. Three busy days each leave at least one common free day across a seven-day week; no arithmetic explanation or calendar calculation is required of students.

**6 — Rooms:** Paz A (quiet and free); Rui B (coffee $1 and music); Sol C (quiet and coffee). A explicitly has no coffee offer, while C’s paid coffee and B’s noisy music distinguish them. The prices belong to fictional club-room offers, not claims about actual local prices.

**7 — False messages:** 1 quiet; 2 cheap; 3 expensive. Full corrected sentences are also acceptable. Price judgments are established within the source’s classroom contrast of $1/$7, not a universal cost standard.

**8 — Familiar place game:** The selected place must be known to both partners. Keep the name private and give three accurate adjective clues. The listener may ask a follow-up question if several familiar places fit. Accept the guessed place only after the speaker confirms it; do not invent an absolute unique answer from three general adjectives. Open clues should use the taught quiet, noisy, busy, beautiful, cheap and expensive where they fit meaning.

## Unit 4 — Pack a picnic for four (page 100)

**1 — Lunchboxes:** Kim B; Ari C; Ben D; Lee A. Supporting foods: B bread/cheese/apple; C rice/eggs/apple; D bread/cheese/banana; A rice/fish/tomatoes. Ben’s no-apple condition distinguishes D from B. No count of egg halves, rice grains or tomato pieces is assessed.

**2 — Lunchbox memory:** A rice, fish, tomatoes; B bread, cheese, apple; C rice, eggs, apple; D bread, cheese, banana. The chosen box stays private until the partner identifies it from all three foods. Singular/plural equivalents that preserve the pictured food type are acceptable. This retrieves whole food sets rather than renaming the old lesson cutout sheet.

**3 — Drinks:** milk; tea and coffee; juice. Initial letters are supplied; assess the completed whole words, not whether a learner repeats the printed first letter.

**4 — Flavor round:** lemon = sour; chips with salt = salty; chili pepper = spicy; ice cream with sugar = sweet. The food choice is private. The partner asks up to three be questions about flavor, then guesses the food. These are the depicted menu choices in an ordinary classroom example, not a rule about every preparation of those foods. No actual tasting or new food vocabulary is required. Independent written answers: lemon sour; chips salty.

## Unit 4 — Fix the recipe. Share the food. (page 101)

**5 — Recipe repair:** A Add; B Mix; C Wash; D Cut. Each incorrect underlined verb is replaced; the native tool symbol supplies supporting action evidence. Accept lowercase target verbs if spelling is otherwise accurate, though the repaired imperatives normally start with a capital.

**6 — Recipe relay:** Model C, D, A, B: wash tomatoes, cut tomatoes into small pieces, add oil to the bowl, mix everything with a spoon. Also accept A, C, D, B or C, A, D, B: oil can go into the bowl before or between washing and cutting. Keep washing before cutting and mix everything last. The partner verifies verb/action agreement and the supported logical order. Independent alternative: say all four repaired steps from memory and uncover/check. No new recording or actual food preparation is required.

**7 — Rescue labels:** waste; share; free, in left-to-right order. Good bread going into a bin represents waste; giving bread to a friend represents sharing; $0 supplies the price word free. These are fictional usable-food examples, not instructions to share unsafe food.

**8 — New notice:** An original comprehensible notice using share and free and naming food, place and time. Example: I want to share bread. It is free. Art Club, today at four. Simpler concise notices are acceptable if all required information is clear. The reader must correctly report what food, where and when, then the writer confirms the match. No copied sentence frame, compulsory personal fact or actual posting/handover is required.

## Revision method and visual-resource record

The nine pages use retrieval, editing, private choices, routing, source-based selection, memory and practical message checks. They do not repeat the main-book alphabet/number panels, body picture, job portraits, food/clothes catalogs or action photographs. Three fresh picture resources support distinct games: seven table objects (V2), a four-panel plan (V3), and four lunchboxes (V4). All other clue cards, lockers, clocks, color trails, calendars, recipe tools and notices are original editable native resources. Role/source cards are not decorative boxes around ordinary drills. First pages 93, 96, 98 and 100 reserve at least 32mm at the bottom of the content area for the future Blooket QR slot; the parent integration process owns that placeholder and any future link.
