**3.** 1 after; 2 before. The diagram explicitly fixes breakfast at 07:00 and walking to work at 07:30.

**1.** 1 too many; 2 not enough; 3 too much.

**2.** 1 We have enough rice. 2 We do not have enough tomatoes. The first is an affirmative amount and the second a shortage; both place enough immediately before the noun. Alternative order is not expected because the task asks for the supplied words.

## 4D — Food for everyone! · page 88

**1.** 1 because; 2 so.

**2.** I have too many eggs, so I share them. Do you want two? Required features are initial capitals, capital I, a comma before so, a full stop after them and a question mark after two.

**3.** A short comprehensible food-sharing message naming the food and giving a reason for sharing. An accurate because-clause or a reason followed by , so is appropriate. Example: I have too many apples, so I share them. Do you want two? Check appropriate capitalization, a full stop and any question mark. Assess successful meaning and target form rather than a compulsory copied model.

# Vocabulary practice — lessons 1A–4D

## 1A — New friends · pages 101–102

**1.** A Good morning. B Good afternoon. C Good evening. D Goodbye. / See you later. The instruction explicitly asks for morning, afternoon or evening in A–C, so Hello alone does not complete that controlled requirement. In the partner round, the speaker chooses a card privately; the listener identifies it from the spoken response. Change roles. No recording or teacher-only hidden resource is required.

**2.** A K-I-M, Kim. B L-U-Z, Luz. C S-A-M, Sam. D I-D-A, Ida. The caller spells the letters; the listener writes the name and reports the matching card letter. The independent alternative retrieves two names from memory and spells the learner’s own name. Do not assess whether a name is culturally familiar, only the letters shown/heard.

**3.** A 4–0–8. B 2–10–6. C 9–1–5. D 7–3–4. Each is a three-number code, not necessarily a three-digit code; ten correctly occupies one large box. A reader chooses privately; the listener covers the written code words and identifies the locker from the spoken numbers. For the invented-code extension, three correctly spelled number words from zero to ten must agree with the partner’s digits and read-back. No addition, subtraction or other arithmetic is assessed.

## 1B — What's your job? · pages 103–104

**3.** 1 smart; 2 fun; 3 friendly; 4 famous.

No copied sentence frame, compulsory personal fact or actual posting/handover is required.

# Practical language — answer key

## C5 · Check it at the checkout

1. 1 Yes, we do. 2 No, thanks. 3 Yes, of course. 4 It’s four dollars.
2. Change Milk to Apples. The prices and total stay $3, $2, $5; this is a communication/receipt check, not a new arithmetic test. Accept: Excuse me. I have rice and apples. The receipt says milk. / Can you change milk to apples, please?
3. Open. Customer keeps a three-food list private; shopkeeper keeps four stocked foods/prices/payment methods private. Check item availability, chosen alternatives, actual prices and accepted payment, then the final receipt. Invented information is appropriate. Neither role sees the other’s choices before speaking.

## C6 · A visitor in your town

The map is original classroom fiction. North is up; the start arrow faces east along Main Street. Library/café, park/cinema and museum/pool are opposite pairs. Oak Street is crossed before Pine Street from the start. Today the museum is closed; all five other marked places are open. Opposite is additional scaffolded vocabulary: the visible café/library model and map show its meaning before the questions.
1. 1 left; 2 opposite; 3 opposite; 4 left.
2. 1 No, you can’t. It’s closed today. 2 At the library. / You can read at the library. 3 Go straight along Main Street. The café is on your right, before Oak Street. Accept equivalent supported directions; do not accept left/opposite side. Full sentences not compulsory for items1–2.
3. Open: library for reading, pool for swimming, cinema for a movie, café for food, park for walking. Learner chooses two goals privately. The helper must offer open places and a geometrically correct route. A partner’s drawing/repetition checks the route. Accept routes using either side of a marked street when destination and turn directions agree.

## C7 · A day worth writing about

1. 1 The café was closed. 2 They saw a music show and ate in the park / they enjoyed a different activity together. Any supported reason.
2. Yesterday, I went to the park. Then I saw my friend. Also accept Yesterday I went to the park. Then I saw my friend. Require capital Yesterday/I/Then, went, and two sentence-ending full stops; a comma after Yesterday is optional.
3. Open. A real or invented day, date and title, a comprehensible small event, events in order, appropriate past verbs and at least two sequence words. Seven7mm lines are provided. Do not require a dramatic event or every sequence word. Reader supplies a plausible alternative title and a genuine question; revise for clarity.

## C8 · The right ticket

1. 1 c; 2 a; 3 d; 4 b.
2. That’s a return ticket to Bristol on Friday at ten thirty. Forty-five dollars. Equivalent wording accepted; three corrected details are return, Friday and10:30. City and the $45 price were already correct. The corrected choice agrees with the task3 timetable.
3. Open. Friday/Bristol, one of the three supplied departure times, correct ticket type and corresponding price.09:15 one way$25 / return$40;10:30 one way$28 / return$45;14:00 one way$22 / return$36. Invented passenger name. Confirm all six details and respond appropriately to a request for repetition or spelling. Role choice stays private; the timetable itself is shared.


### 8A reference expansion — travel practice2

Learners choose three chunks from the fuller bank and describe what they do on a trip, using familiar present simple. The listener records the three verbs and checks them with the speaker; then swap. Original controlled ticket answers remain unchanged. Bank-only alternatives include book a room, pack a bag, buy a ticket, catch a bus, get on a bus and get off a bus. Do not require unmodeled irregular past forms; this is a general travel-habit task, not past narrative. Solo learners cover both word bank and tickets before retrieving three chunks.
