HORIZONS A1 — LESSON 2D — ANSWERS AND TEACHER NOTES
IS THAT MY PHONE? · Pages 19–20

1. Colors, in printed/audio order: red, blue, green, yellow, black, white,
   brown, orange, gray, pink. Pointing/repetition; no written answer.

2. Leo's phone is black. It is small. He rejects the white phone.

3. 1 after: the item follows the adjective in a white phone.
   2 after: the adjective follows is in My phone is black.
   3 before: the item precedes the adjective in un teléfono negro.
   Guide noticing orally; no written response needed.

   English color adjectives precede a noun: a black phone. In the Spanish
   equivalent they follow it: un teléfono negro. After a form of be,
   the adjective follows the verb: My phone is black / Mi teléfono es negro.
   Use the printed Spanish examples as a specific comparison, not a rule
   that every Spanish adjective always follows a noun. Multilingual groups
   may compare the same two frames with their own language orally.
   This lesson teaches adjective position relative to the noun and be;
   it does not teach the relative order of multiple adjectives.

4. 1 It's a black phone. (Printed example.)
   2 The wallet is red.
   3 It's a green bottle.
   4 My headphones are black.

5. Students' own items and answers. Use What color is your ...? and
   Is it big or small? Keep answers factual.

6. Claim-to-item decisions: Ana = 4 (red, big wallet); Luis = 6 (blue,
   big bottle); Mia = 7 (yellow, small umbrella). Both colour and size are
   visible in the paired photographs. Inventory identities remain:
   1 black/small phone; 2 white/big phone; 3 brown/small wallet;
   4 red/big wallet; 5 green/small bottle; 6 blue/big bottle;
   7 yellow/small umbrella; 8 orange/big umbrella.
   Learners find the numbered item from each person's description, rather
   than only naming/describing the same visible objects. No title is added
   to this inventory and the canonical number font/colour is retained.

7. Information gap / Lost and Found transaction.
   Student A privately chooses an item from the inventory without revealing
   its number. Student B asks for the item, color and size, identifies the
   number, confirms ownership and completes the office record. The teacher
   establishes the private-choice routine without extra printed procedure.
   Example: name = student's name; item = bottle; color = green;
   big/small = small; number = 5. Students then exchange roles and choose
   a different item. Success = the description and selected number agree.

8. Real-object ownership task. Prepare three familiar items without revealing
   their owners to the guesser (the teacher may collect/arrange them before
   the exchange). Ask classmates using the model; record each owner's name
   beside the corresponding numbered real item, then return the right item.
   Items or fictional ownership may be chosen for classroom convenience.
   This is not a printed A/B table with both answers visible. Recycle 2A
   demonstratives and 2C possessives; no mine/yours or past-tense account is
   needed. Success means that ownership and identification agree.

Extra practice: write two descriptive sentences about a household item,
then let a family member guess. Keep the item name private initially;
colour/size clues supply the identifying evidence. An imagined household
exchange is acceptable; no external message is required.

Language progression: personal items and demonstratives (2A), a/an (1B,
2B), possession/name's (2C), big/old/new (1C) and small (2C) are recycled.
Main new targets: color vocabulary, What color is your ...?, adjective
position before a noun and after be, compared with Spanish examples.
Lost and Found is supported by the scene;
terminal and office are transparent Spanish cognates. No possessive
pronouns (mine/yours), past-tense loss story, present-simple questions
or advanced claim-office terminology are required.
