# Horizons A1 — Guided Discovery Methodology

**Status: CANONICAL / PRIMARY PEDAGOGICAL METHOD**

This document defines the central teaching methodology of the Horizons Student's Book. It applies to new lesson authoring, completion of unfinished legacy lessons, Grammar Reference/Practice, Vocabulary Practice, Unit Reviews, and the design of communicative tasks.

The visual design system must support this methodology rather than compete with it.

## 1. Core principle

**Horizons is built primarily around Guided Discovery.**

Students should normally encounter the target language **in meaningful context before they are given an explicit rule**. They see, hear, read, compare and use examples that contain the target grammar, vocabulary or functional language. The lesson then asks explicit questions that guide them to notice how the language works.

The aim is not to make students guess blindly. The context must provide enough evidence for an A1 learner to observe a pattern, compare examples, test an idea and arrive at a usable understanding with guidance.

The normal direction is:

**context → noticing → guided analysis → clarification → controlled practice → communicative use → transfer to a real situation**

An explicit explanation may still appear, but it should usually **confirm, organize or clarify what students have already examined**, rather than replace the discovery process.

## 2. Language first appears in context

Target language should be introduced through situations that make its meaning and purpose visible.

Appropriate contexts include:

- conversations;
- short readings;
- listening extracts;
- profiles;
- forms;
- messages and chats;
- reviews;
- menus;
- schedules;
- maps;
- advertisements;
- registration screens;
- tickets;
- interviews;
- social posts;
- workplace interactions;
- other recognizable real-world texts and situations.

The context should not be decorative. It should contain the evidence students need for the language work that follows.

For example, if students are learning capitalization for personal information, they should first see names, cities, countries and form entries written naturally inside a realistic form or exchange. They can then analyze what is capitalized and why.

## 3. Explicit discovery questions are required

Guided Discovery in Horizons is **not** simply presenting examples and hoping students notice the pattern.

Lessons should include explicit questions or micro-tasks that direct attention to form, meaning, use or word order.

Useful discovery prompts at A1 include questions such as:

- Look at the examples. Which word changes?
- Which form goes with **I**?
- Which form goes with **he / she**?
- Is the sentence affirmative, negative or a question?
- What comes first in the question?
- Which words describe a person? Which words describe a place?
- Do we use **a** or **an** before this word? Look at the sound.
- Which words have capital letters? Why?
- Which information is the same in both examples?
- What does the speaker want to know?
- Which phrase is used to ask for a phone number?
- Compare the two sentences. What is different?

The wording must remain simple enough for A1 students. Avoid unnecessary metalanguage when a direct observation question can do the job.

## 4. Discovery must be evidence-based

Students should never be asked to discover a rule from insufficient or misleading evidence.

A good Guided Discovery sequence:

1. provides several clear examples when comparison is needed;
2. visually or verbally directs attention to the relevant language;
3. asks manageable questions one step at a time;
4. lets students compare answers, preferably with a partner when useful;
5. confirms the pattern with a concise rule, table or summary when necessary;
6. immediately gives students a chance to test the discovered rule.

Do not turn Guided Discovery into a puzzle whose answer depends on knowledge the student has not yet encountered.

## 5. Short-exercise learning cycle

Horizons favors short exercises, but the exercises must form a connected discovery sequence.

A typical language-focused progression is:

1. **Encounter** — students read/listen/look at contextualized language.
2. **Notice** — students identify examples, differences or repeated forms.
3. **Analyze** — explicit questions lead students toward the pattern or use.
4. **Clarify** — a concise rule, table, vocabulary grouping or teacher-supported summary confirms the discovery.
5. **Practice** — students complete a short controlled task.
6. **Compare** — students check or discuss answers with a partner when appropriate.
7. **Use** — students use the language to exchange information, make a choice, solve a small task or communicate something personal.
8. **Transfer** — the language appears in a realistic situation or productive outcome.

Not every lesson needs eight separately numbered exercises. Several stages can exist inside one exercise. What matters is the learning progression.

## 6. Lesson-letter application

### Lesson A — Grammar and/or Vocabulary

Lesson A should usually introduce its main grammar or vocabulary through context, then lead students through noticing and guided analysis before controlled and communicative practice.

A grammar box should normally **confirm a discovery**, not function as the first and only exposure to the rule.

### Lesson B — Reading/Listening with Vocabulary

Lesson B is receptive-skill led. Students first understand the text or audio, but Guided Discovery can still operate through:

- noticing useful vocabulary in context;
- inferring meaning from surrounding information;
- identifying repeated phrases;
- comparing how speakers express information;
- discussing what a phrase means or when it is used.

The text/listening remains central.

### Lesson C — Grammar and/or Vocabulary

Lesson C follows the same Guided Discovery philosophy as Lesson A but may use a different context, page composition or discovery mechanism.

It should not default to explanation → worksheet. Students should encounter and analyze the target language before or while the rule is formalized.

### Lesson D — Speaking or Writing through real situations

Lesson D is where the methodology reaches its most practical form.

**Lesson D should normally culminate in a believable real-world situation, simulation or role-play rather than a purely classroom-style production exercise.**

Students should have:

- a role;
- a reason to communicate;
- information to obtain, give or negotiate;
- a realistic text, object or interface to use;
- a concrete outcome.

The final task should make the target language useful for accomplishing something.

Examples include:

- hotel check-in;
- joining a gym;
- registering for a course;
- buying a ticket;
- ordering or purchasing something;
- asking for directions;
- handling a lost-and-found situation;
- completing an application;
- making an appointment;
- responding to a customer or visitor;
- creating or using a simple business service.

## 7. Canonical Lesson 1D model: a form as a real transaction

Lesson 1D, **LET'S FILL A FORM!**, should not be treated as "students fill in a worksheet about personal information."

The intended methodology is a real-situation simulation.

Students can create or choose a simple business or service, for example:

- a gym;
- a hotel;
- a language school;
- a club;
- another appropriate A1 context.

One student temporarily represents the business or service. A classmate is the customer, guest, member or new student.

The interaction can then require students to:

1. examine a realistic model form and discover how personal information and capitalization work;
2. identify the questions needed to obtain the information;
3. create or select their business/service context;
4. role-play the real interaction;
5. ask the classmate for the required personal information;
6. listen and record the classmate's actual or safely invented information;
7. complete the relevant registration/check-in/membership form;
8. check capitalization and completeness;
9. swap roles or compare completed forms.

The form is therefore **the product of a communicative transaction**, not the exercise itself.

The exact business context may vary; the important element is that the learner has a credible reason to ask for and record another person's information.

## 8. Pairwork is part of Guided Discovery

Pairwork is not limited to the final speaking activity.

It can support discovery itself. Students may:

- compare what they noticed;
- agree on which rule fits the examples;
- check classifications;
- explain why they selected a form;
- test a question with a partner;
- exchange missing information;
- rehearse before the real-situation task.

This makes pairwork both **cognitive** and **communicative**.

Avoid pairwork where two students merely read identical answers aloud. A pair task should normally involve comparison, retrieval, information exchange, choice, prediction, checking or a real communicative goal.

## 9. Real-world texts and interfaces must be functional

Horizons frequently uses real-world-looking forms, chats, maps, schedules, reviews, menus, tickets and interfaces.

These should not exist only because they look contemporary.

A real-world artifact should normally be something the learner must **read, interpret, complete, respond to or use during a task**.

Examples:

- a hotel form is completed after a check-in exchange;
- a map is used to give or follow directions;
- a review is analyzed before students write or discuss their own opinion;
- a timetable is used to ask about times;
- a lost-and-found record is used to identify and claim an object;
- a menu supports an ordering interaction.

The visual authenticity and the pedagogical function should reinforce each other.

## 10. Controlled practice still matters

Guided Discovery does not eliminate explicit instruction or controlled practice.

A1 learners still need short activities that stabilize the pattern after discovery, such as:

- choosing;
- matching;
- completing;
- ordering;
- categorizing;
- correcting;
- transforming;
- copying a useful model with a meaningful change.

These tasks should prepare students for the later communicative task rather than become the entire lesson.

More concentrated grammar/vocabulary consolidation can continue in Grammar Reference/Practice and Vocabulary Practice.

## 11. Grammar Reference/Practice and Vocabulary Practice

The back-of-book practice sections should reinforce the same methodology but may be more explicit and concentrated.

### Grammar Reference/Practice

A useful sequence is:

**examples from context → guided reminder/question → concise rule/table → controlled practice → application**

The section remains an activity section, not just a grammar explanation.

### Vocabulary Practice

Vocabulary Practice should recycle words through recognition, categorization, association, contextual use and communication.

Whenever practical, vocabulary should reappear in a situation or pair task rather than remain isolated as a list.

## 12. Design requirements created by the methodology

The visual system must make the learning path legible.

Pages should make it easy to see:

- the contextual input;
- the language evidence students should inspect;
- the explicit discovery question(s);
- the clarification or rule that follows;
- the short practice task;
- the pairwork stage;
- the final real-world outcome.

Important language may be highlighted through typography, color, spacing or controlled annotation, but the page should not give away every discovery before students have a chance to analyze it.

For Lesson D, the final situation or artifact should carry substantial visual weight. The page should feel like preparation for a real interaction, not a sequence of disconnected workbook questions.

## 13. Authoring test for every new lesson

Before approving a lesson, check:

1. **Where does the student first encounter the target language in context?**
2. **What evidence can the student analyze?**
3. **What explicit question guides the discovery?**
4. **Where is the discovery confirmed or clarified?**
5. **What short practice lets the student test it?**
6. **Where does pairwork add genuine value?**
7. **What does the student ultimately do with the language?**
8. **If this is Lesson D, what real situation, role or practical outcome makes the language necessary?**

If a lesson begins with a rule, follows with unrelated exercises and ends with generic "talk to your partner" instructions, it does not yet reflect the intended Horizons methodology.

## 14. Priority rule

When a visual idea, activity type or page-layout preference conflicts with the Guided Discovery sequence, **the pedagogy wins**.

The design exists to make discovery, analysis, practice and real communication clearer and more engaging.
