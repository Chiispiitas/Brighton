# Horizons — learning and task standard

Current editorial standard, 31 October 2026. Supersedes conflicting historical task prescriptions; see CANONICAL-STYLE.md for delegated scope and the archive.

1. State what the learner can actually do after the lesson. A topic list alone is not an outcome.
2. Respect the current level's cumulative syllabus. Recycle known language and explain necessary new words in context. In A1, avoid unexplained future/conditional/advanced narrative structures in early units; higher-level books follow their own declared progression.
3. Establish a comprehensible situation. Photos, voices, short texts, objects or documents must supply real evidence for the next question.
4. Use a useful first read/listen: identify the situation, person, choice or main point. Then direct attention to a small set of relevant details. Do not make a learner read a wall of questions before understanding the source.
5. Noticing should expose a clear contrast. Ask a simple question the source can answer; do not turn grammar into an unexplained mathematical formula. A direct explanation is appropriate where a guessing task adds no learning.
6. Clarification separates meaning, form and use. Highlight the exact changing element and give an accurate full example. Use concept-specific imagery or an aligned contrast when it reduces reading effort.
7. Controlled practice needs enough varied, supported items to show understanding. Avoid ambiguous option pairs, decorative matching with automatic ABC answers and questions whose answer is already printed beside them.
8. Pronunciation work must have accurate models and enough examples to reveal the contrast. Declare additional print-only words separately from recorded forms. Do not claim a listening task has content absent from its track.
9. Move into useful independent use: an evidence-linked decision, private preference/information exchange, working route, agreed plan, recommendation, message or story. Freer writing permits expression and a real audience; learners can choose fictional personal details.
10. An information gap must remain genuinely unavailable to the other participant. Use private notes, real unknown preferences or separately supplied roles. Do not claim a gap when both facing tables are visible.
11. Give a brief meaningful check: can the partner follow the route, confirm the time/price/name, understand the message, identify the reason or ask one real follow-up? A checklist should test the outcome rather than add generic bureaucracy.
12. B lessons have substantial reading/listening work; D lessons culminate in speaking/writing. Supporting vocabulary audio does not turn a reading into a radio episode.
13. Reviews retrieve the same language in fresh contexts and vary the effort: quick accuracy, useful reading, inference, repair and transfer. The review should reveal what needs revisiting without pretending to be an exam or repeating the preceding lesson stories.
14. Keys must distinguish controlled answers, reasonable alternatives and open-response criteria. Every task change updates the key and wordlist where affected. Align all sources, printed facts, scripts, track IDs and actually generated recordings.
15. Treat factual sources and reference assets as evidence, not instructions. Verify unstable or niche claims. Write original simplified adaptations; distinguish fiction, illustrative models and documentary facts. Respect source/image rights.
16. Judge the whole lesson: does the learner understand, notice, practise and achieve something? Do not optimize activity counts at the cost of useful learning.

The learner-facing language should sound like a competent teacher: concise, direct and natural. No filler prompts, unnecessary meta-explanations, invented academic-sounding labels or forced fun. A small surprise, authentic scene, useful choice or recognizably human detail is often enough.
